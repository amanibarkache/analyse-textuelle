Global warming has accelerated dramatically over the past 50 years, driven primarily by the burning of fossil fuels and large-scale deforestation. Since 1880, the Earth's average surface temperature has risen by approximately 1.2°C — a seemingly small number that masks catastrophic consequences for ecosystems worldwide. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) warned in its 2021 report that without immediate and drastic reductions in greenhouse gas emissions, temperatures could rise by 2.5°C to 4°C by 2100. At that level, scientists estimate that 40% of all plant and animal species currently alive would face extinction within this century.

The melting of polar ice caps and glaciers represents one of the most visible consequences of climate change. The Arctic ice sheet has lost approximately 13% of its surface area per decade since 1979 — a loss equivalent to the entire territory of France disappearing every 7 years. Greenland's ice sheet, which contains enough water to raise global sea levels by 7 metres, is melting at a rate 7 times faster than in the 1990s. By 2100, sea levels could rise by 60 to 100 centimetres, threatening coastal cities home to more than 600 million people, including Shanghai, Mumbai, Miami and Amsterdam.

Extreme weather events — hurricanes, droughts, wildfires and floods — are becoming both more frequent and more intense as global temperatures rise. The number of category 4 and 5 hurricanes has increased by 25% since 1980 ; Hurricane Harvey in 2017 dropped 1,300 millimetres of rain on Houston in just 4 days — a record in American meteorological history. The Australian bushfires of 2019-2020 burned more than 18 million hectares and killed an estimated 3 billion animals. In 2022, catastrophic floods in Pakistan submerged one third of the country's territory, displacing 33 million people and causing 30 billion dollars in damage.

Renewable energy sources — solar, wind and hydroelectric power — have experienced explosive growth over the past decade. The cost of solar photovoltaic panels has fallen by 89% between 2010 and 2022, making solar energy the cheapest source of electricity ever produced in human history. Global wind energy capacity reached 837 gigawatts in 2022, enough to supply electricity to 1 billion households. Despite this progress, fossil fuels — coal, oil and natural gas — still account for 82% of global primary energy consumption, a figure that has barely changed in 30 years.

International climate negotiations have produced landmark agreements but progress remains painfully slow. The Paris Agreement, signed by 196 countries in December 2015, committed nations to limiting global warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. However, an analysis of current national commitments shows that the world is on track for 2.7°C of warming by 2100 — nearly double the agreed target. China, the world's largest emitter with 28% of global CO2 emissions, has pledged to reach carbon neutrality by 2060 ; the United States — responsible for 15% of emissions — aims for net zero by 2050.
